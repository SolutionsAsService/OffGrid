Yes. And because you’ve already been working with **12/24 V batteries, an MPPT controller, an inverter, and RV loads**, I’d build this as a practical **RV off-grid electrical manual** rather than a generic solar tutorial.

One important point first: **don’t wire the system from a generic diagram without checking the exact voltage/current ratings printed on your panels, batteries, MPPT, inverter, and RV.** A 2,000 W inverter on 12 V can pull roughly **167 A**, while the same inverter on 24 V is roughly **83 A** before losses. That difference is enormous for cable and fuse sizing. ([Solar RV Panels][1])

Also, solar/battery wiring can start fires or cause severe burns/arc faults. If you're uncertain about a high-current connection, have an RV electrician inspect it before energizing it.

# RV OFF-GRID SOLAR SYSTEM — COMPLETE HOOKUP GUIDE

## 1. The basic architecture

Think of the system as **four separate electrical sections**:

```text
                  SOLAR ARRAY
              ┌─────┬─────┬─────┐
              │     │     │     │
            PANEL PANEL PANEL PANEL
              └─────┴─────┴─────┘
                       │
                  PV DISCONNECT
                       │
                  PV FUSE/BREAKER
                       │
                       ▼
                ┌──────────────┐
                │ MPPT CHARGE   │
                │  CONTROLLER   │
                └──────────────┘
                   │        │
              +    │        │    -
              │    │        │
           DC FUSE │        │
              │    │        │
              ▼    ▼        ▼
        ┌────────────────────────┐
        │      BATTERY BANK      │
        │                        │
        │  + ─────────────── -   │
        └────────────────────────┘
              │
          MAIN FUSE
              │
          DISCONNECT
              │
              ▼
        ┌──────────────┐
        │   INVERTER   │
        │  12/24→120V  │
        └──────────────┘
              │
              ▼
        RV AC LOADS
```

And separately:

```text
BATTERY BANK
     │
     ▼
DC FUSE PANEL
     │
     ├── RV lights
     ├── water pump
     ├── refrigerator controls
     ├── USB/12V outlets
     ├── fans
     └── other 12V loads
```

That separation is important. Your **inverter should not be the source for every little 12 V load**. Small DC loads should come through a properly fused DC distribution panel. Victron's current RV guidance similarly recommends busbars and a fused DC distribution system for loads such as lights, pumps and refrigerators. ([Victron Energy][2])

# 2. THE COMPONENTS YOU NEED

## Solar side

You need:

* Solar panels
* MC4-compatible PV cable
* MC4 connectors
* PV disconnect/breaker
* PV fuse(s), when required by the array configuration
* MPPT charge controller

The panels **must not be connected directly to the batteries**. The MPPT sits between them and converts the panel voltage into the appropriate battery charging voltage. ([Victron Energy][3])

---

# 3. Charge controller

For an RV, I'd strongly favor:

### MPPT

over a basic PWM controller.

The controller has two completely different sides:

```text
PV INPUT                 BATTERY OUTPUT

PV + ────────┐       ┌──────── + Battery
             │       │
             ▼       ▼
          ┌─────────────┐
          │    MPPT     │
          │ CONTROLLER  │
          └─────────────┘
             ▲       ▲
             │       │
PV - ────────┘       └──────── - Battery
```

The **PV voltage** and **battery voltage** do not have to be the same.

That's one of the biggest advantages of MPPT.

For example:

```text
Solar array:
80 V
10 A

MPPT

Battery:
14 V
~50 A
```

The controller converts the higher PV voltage into lower battery voltage/higher current.

---

# 4. PANEL WIRING

There are two fundamental ways to connect panels.

## Parallel

```text
Panel 1 + ─────┐
Panel 2 + ─────┼──── + PV
Panel 3 + ─────┤
Panel 4 + ─────┘

Panel 1 - ─────┐
Panel 2 - ─────┼──── - PV
Panel 3 - ─────┤
Panel 4 - ─────┘
```

Voltage stays approximately the same.

Current adds.

### Advantages

* Better with partial shading
* Lower PV voltage
* Potentially easier compatibility with some controllers

### Disadvantage

More current means larger PV wiring.

---

# 5. Series panels

```text
Panel 1 + ─── Panel 2 -
Panel 2 + ─── Panel 3 -
Panel 3 + ─── Panel 4 -
```

The voltages add.

Current stays approximately the same.

For example, four panels each having:

**Vmp = 20 V**

could produce approximately:

**80 Vmp**

in series.

That's particularly useful when the MPPT is designed for higher PV voltage.

### BUT:

You must calculate the **cold-weather Voc** of the array.

Do not simply add the panel labels and assume you're safe.

The maximum PV voltage of the controller must be greater than the array's **worst-case cold Voc**.

---

# 6. YOUR MPPT HAS A HARD LIMIT

Suppose your controller says:

```text
150 V maximum PV
60 A maximum output
```

Your solar array has to remain below:

**150 V maximum PV input**

under the manufacturer's specified conditions.

And the controller can't exceed:

**60 A charging output.**

Don't design right on the edge if you don't have to.

---

# 7. PANEL → MPPT PROTECTION

A typical arrangement is:

```text
PANELS
  │
  ▼
PV COMBINER
  │
  ├── String fuse
  │
  ▼
PV DISCONNECT
  │
  ▼
MPPT
```

If you have multiple parallel strings, individual string protection may be required.

Fuse sizing depends on the panel/string **Isc** and the applicable equipment/manufacturer requirements. Don't blindly install a 20 A fuse because you saw it in another RV. ([Solar RV Panels][1])

---

# 8. MPPT → BATTERY

This is one of the most important wires in the system.

Example:

```text
MPPT + 
   │
   │
 [FUSE]
   │
   ▼
BATTERY +
```

Negative:

```text
MPPT -
   │
   ▼
NEGATIVE BUSBAR
   │
   ▼
BATTERY -
```

The controller-to-battery cable needs to handle the controller's **maximum output current**.

For example:

### 40 A MPPT

You might have:

```text
40 A controller
↓
appropriately sized cable
↓
50 A fuse/breaker
↓
battery
```

But the actual wire size must be calculated from **current + one-way length + allowable voltage drop + cable insulation/temperature rating**, not simply copied from this example.

The fuse protects the wiring from a fault; it isn't merely there to protect the controller. ([Smart RV Hub][4])

---

# 9. BATTERY BANK

This is where your previous 12/24 V setup becomes extremely important.

You can configure batteries as:

### Parallel

```text
12V 100Ah
   │
   ├─────────┐
   │         │
12V 100Ah   │
   │         │
   └─────────┘

= 12V 200Ah
```

Or:

### Series

```text
12V 100Ah
   +
   │
   │
   -
12V 100Ah

= 24V 100Ah
```

Four 12 V batteries in series:

```text
12V + 12V + 12V + 12V

= 48V
```

Four in a 2S2P arrangement:

```text
12V + 12V
     │
     │
12V + 12V

= 24V with twice the Ah of one string
```

**Every battery must be appropriate for series/parallel operation according to its manufacturer.**

Don't mix:

* old and new batteries
* different chemistries
* substantially different capacities
* batteries with different states of health

unless the manufacturer specifically permits it.

---

# 10. BATTERY BALANCING

If you're running multiple batteries in parallel, don't just do:

```text
Battery 1 → Battery 2 → Battery 3 → Battery 4
```

and expect perfect current sharing.

A better approach is a properly designed busbar arrangement.

For example:

```text
          + BUSBAR
          │
    ┌─────┼─────┬─────┐
    │     │     │     │
   B1    B2    B3    B4
    │     │     │     │
    └─────┼─────┴─────┘
          │
       - BUSBAR
```

Victron specifically recommends organized busbar/distribution arrangements for battery systems. ([Victron Energy][2])

---

# 11. MAIN BATTERY FUSE

This is **not optional**.

The inverter can potentially pull hundreds of amps.

Your main arrangement should look like:

```text
BATTERY +
    │
    │ VERY SHORT
    ▼
[MAIN FUSE]
    │
    ▼
[MASTER DISCONNECT]
    │
    ▼
+ BUSBAR
```

Then:

```text
+ BUSBAR
   │
   ├──────────── MPPT
   │
   ├──────────── DC FUSE PANEL
   │
   └──────────── INVERTER
```

The main fuse should be installed close to the battery positive terminal.

For high-current inverter circuits, appropriate high-interrupt-capacity fuses such as Class T or appropriately rated ANL/MRBF systems may be used depending on the installation. ([Solar RV Panels][1])

---

# 12. INVERTER

This is where the system gets dangerous because the current becomes enormous.

Suppose:

### 2,000 W inverter

At 12 V:

**2,000 ÷ 12 ≈ 167 A**

At 24 V:

**2,000 ÷ 24 ≈ 83 A**

And that's before inverter losses.

So:

### 12 V system

requires extremely heavy battery cabling.

### 24 V system

cuts the current roughly in half.

That's why I would seriously consider **24 V for a larger RV solar system**, assuming your RV's existing DC equipment and inverter architecture are compatible.

# 13. INVERTER CONNECTION

```text
BATTERY +

     │
     ▼
 [MAIN FUSE]
     │
     ▼
 [DISCONNECT]
     │
     ▼
 + BUSBAR
     │
     │
     ▼
 INVERTER +
 
BATTERY -
     │
     ▼
 NEGATIVE BUSBAR
     │
     ▼
 INVERTER -
```

Keep the battery-to-inverter cables:

**short**

**large enough**

**properly crimped**

**properly supported**

**protected from abrasion**

A loose high-current connection can generate extreme heat.

Victron specifically warns that battery connections must be secure and that improper wiring can result in fire, equipment damage or injury. ([Victron Energy][5])

# 14. DC RV LOADS

Your RV probably already has a 12 V DC distribution system.

Things such as:

* lights
* water pump
* furnace controls
* refrigerator controls
* fans
* USB outlets
* propane appliances' electronics
* control boards

can remain on the DC side.

Conceptually:

```text
BATTERY
   │
   ▼
+ BUSBAR
   │
   ▼
[MAIN DC FUSE]
   │
   ▼
DC FUSE PANEL
   │
   ├── 5A → lights
   ├── 10A → USB
   ├── 15A → pump
   ├── 15A → refrigerator
   ├── etc.
```

**Do not randomly replace the RV's existing fuses with larger fuses.**

The fuse protects the circuit wiring.

---

# 15. AC SIDE

Your inverter produces:

```text
12/24 V DC
       ↓
   INVERTER
       ↓
    120 V AC
```

That AC output should go into a properly designed AC distribution system.

For a permanent RV installation:

```text
INVERTER
   │
   ▼
AC BREAKER/DISCONNECT
   │
   ▼
RV AC PANEL
   │
   ├── outlets
   ├── TV
   ├── computer
   ├── refrigerator
   └── other loads
```

The neutral/ground arrangement is **inverter-specific**. Some inverters switch/bond neutral internally; others don't.

**Do not randomly bond neutral to ground.**

That is one of the areas where you should follow the exact inverter manual and RV electrical configuration.

Victron's RV guidance emphasizes proper grounding of the inverter, AC distribution and DC system. ([Victron Energy][2])

---

# 16. SHORE POWER

If your RV already has shore power:

```text
          SHORE POWER
               │
               ▼
         RV AC PANEL
               │
               ├──── RV loads
               │
               ▼
       INVERTER/CHARGER
               │
               ▼
          BATTERY BANK
```

If you're using a simple inverter rather than an inverter/charger, **don't simply plug the RV's shore-power cord into an outlet powered by the inverter without a properly engineered transfer arrangement.**

Otherwise you can create dangerous backfeeding.

Use a proper transfer switch or an inverter designed for the intended RV configuration.

---

# 17. SOLAR + SHORE + ALTERNATOR

A really good RV off-grid system can have **three charging sources**:

```text
                 SOLAR
                   │
                   ▼
                MPPT
                   │
                   │
ALTERNATOR → DC-DC CHARGER → BATTERY
                   │
                   │
SHORE → RV CHARGER ─┘
                   │
                   ▼
              BATTERY BANK
                   │
                   ▼
                INVERTER
```

That is much more resilient than trying to make solar do everything.

You can drive:

**engine alternator → DC-DC charger → batteries**

while driving.

Then:

**solar → MPPT → batteries**

when parked.

Then:

**shore power → charger → batteries**

when you have access to electricity.

# 18. FUSES — THE SIMPLE RULE

Think about every source of energy.

### Solar

```text
PV array
 ↓
PV protection
 ↓
MPPT
```

### MPPT

```text
MPPT
 ↓
battery-side fuse/breaker
 ↓
battery
```

### Battery → inverter

```text
battery
 ↓
HIGH CURRENT FUSE
 ↓
disconnect
 ↓
inverter
```

### DC loads

```text
battery
 ↓
DC fuse panel
 ↓
individual fuses
 ↓
loads
```

The fuse is fundamentally there to prevent a wiring fault from turning the cable into a heating element. ([Smart RV Hub][4])

# 19. WIRE SIZE

Don't memorize one wire gauge.

Calculate it.

You need:

**current**

*

**one-way cable length**

*

**allowable voltage drop**

*

**temperature/installation conditions**

For example, a 200 A inverter circuit and a 20 A solar circuit are completely different.

### Typical conceptual hierarchy:

```text
SOLAR PV
10–12 AWG-ish
        ↓
MPPT → BATTERY
6 AWG-ish for some moderate systems
        ↓
BATTERY → INVERTER
2/0, 4/0 or other very large cable
depending on voltage/current/run
```

Those are **examples, not a prescription**. Actual sizing must be calculated for your equipment.

The battery-to-inverter connection is normally the largest cable in the system because it carries the greatest current. ([Solar RV Panels][1])

# 20. CONNECTION ORDER

This is important.

Different manufacturers can specify different procedures, so **your controller manual wins**.

For many MPPT controllers, a common startup sequence is:

### CONNECT

```text
1. Battery
       ↓
2. MPPT battery connection
       ↓
3. PV
       ↓
4. Inverter
       ↓
5. Loads
```

For example, Victron's equipment is designed to have the battery connected so the controller can detect the battery voltage before PV is applied. ([Victron Energy][6])

### DISCONNECT

Generally:

```text
1. PV
2. Inverter/loads
3. Battery
```

But again:

**follow your specific controller manufacturer's sequence.**

# 21. NEVER DO THESE

### ❌ Panel → battery directly

No.

```text
PANEL ─────── BATTERY
```

Don't do this. A proper solar charge controller belongs between them. ([Victron Energy][3])

### ❌ Battery → inverter without fuse

No.

### ❌ Tiny wire on a 2,000 W inverter

Absolutely not.

### ❌ Fuse bigger than the wire can safely carry

No.

### ❌ Mix 12 V and 24 V equipment accidentally

Very bad.

### ❌ Connect batteries with reversed polarity

Potentially catastrophic.

### ❌ Use automotive AC extension cords for permanent inverter wiring

No.

### ❌ Backfeed campground/RV shore power

Extremely dangerous.

### ❌ Work on exposed PV connectors in full sun

PV panels produce power whenever illuminated.

### ❌ Put batteries in an unventilated/unsafe compartment

Battery chemistry determines ventilation and installation requirements.

I'd also install:

### Battery monitor / shunt

* current
* amp-hours
* charging
* discharging
* state of charge

# 27. THE MOST IMPORTANT THING FOR YOUR CURRENT RV

Because you've previously had **a fuse melt/pop while running things like a mini-fridge, TV and evaporative cooler**, I would **not simply add another larger fuse**.

A melted fuse holder is a warning that something upstream needs investigation:

**overcurrent, undersized wire, poor crimp/connection, inadequate fuse holder, excessive heat, or a combination of these.**

A larger fuse can make the situation worse because the wiring may be the part that's overheating.

For your actual RV, I'd want to calculate the system from the ground up rather than guessing.
